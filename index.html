Saron Graveyard · Troedyrhiw

Recording the lives behind the stones.

Friends of Saron is a volunteer-led project to conserve the historic nonconformist graveyard at Saron, Troedyrhiw — and to tell the stories of those buried here.

About the project

A small Welsh graveyard with a big story.

Saron graveyard sits on the hillside above Troedyrhiw in the Merthyr valley. Established in the early nineteenth century, it became the final resting place of ironworkers, ministers, tradespeople, housewives, children and others from this part of industrial South Wales.

The Friends of Saron group formed in 2009 when local residents became concerned about the deteriorating condition of the site. Since then volunteers have cleared vegetation, repaired boundary walls, stabilised headstones and begun the careful task of recording each burial.

History

From chapel ground to community project.

1820

1820

Early days of the Independent cause in Troedyrhiw

A number of people in the area decided to start a Sunday School with help from Bethesda Chapel in Merthyr. Initially the school and services took place from house to house but later at the Old Mill and the Harp Inn.

1835

1835

Land purchased for Saron

Land is provided by Sir Josiah John Guest of the Dowlais Iron Company, who claimed ownership, for the building of Saron Chapel and the associated burial ground.

1852

1852

Chapel and graveyard expand

Saron Chapel is rebuilt to accommodate a growing congregation drawn from the rapidly expanding mining and ironworking community. The actual landowner was found to be Wyndham Lewis and he granted a lease for this purpose.

1900s

Late 19th – early 20th century

A valley community in stone

Burials reflect the social history of the valley: industrial accidents, epidemics, migration, revival movements and the impact of war are all recorded in the inscriptions.

1960s

1960s – early 2000s

Decline, closure and dereliction

With the decline of heavy industry and changes in patterns of worship, chapel attendance falls. Following the closure of the chapel in 1983 and its subsequent demolition, the burial ground progressively becomes overgrown and the condition of the memorials and boundary walls deteriorates.

2009

2009

Friends of Saron formed

Concerned local residents form the Friends of Saron group to take action. Regular work days begin, and links are made with Merthyr Tydfil County Borough Council, the landowner, the leaseholder and a variety of organisations and individuals potentially capable of providing guidance and support.

Today

Today

Recording and restoration

Volunteers continue the painstaking work of vegetation management, stone conservation and inscription recording. The online Burials Register allows families and researchers around the world to explore Saron’s story.

What we do

Practical care, research and community.

Hands-on conservation

Regular work days see volunteers clearing vegetation, making safe leaning stones, repairing boundaries and improving access paths. All work is done with hand tools and taking great care to avoid damage.

No specialist skills are required – just appropriate clothing and a willingness to help. Training, tools and plenty of tea are provided.

Inscription & archive research

Every legible inscription is transcribed, photographed and cross-referenced with census records, parish registers, nonconformist burial records and Welsh newspaper archives.

This work feeds directly into the public Burials Register — a permanent, searchable record for families tracing their South Wales ancestry.

Community & education

We hold regular open days, guided walks and talks in partnership with local schools, Merthyr Tydfil Borough Council and heritage organisations across South Wales.

Volunteers of all ages and backgrounds are welcome — no experience required. Just bring enthusiasm for Welsh history and comfortable footwear.

Wildlife & wildflower garden

A section of the graveyard has been designated as a managed wildflower habitat, supporting pollinators and providing a peaceful green space for the community.

Volunteer

Come and give us a hand.

Friends of Saron is a small group of local people. Everything you can see on this site — the cleared ground, the reset headstones, the burial records, the photographs — was done by volunteers, most of them in their own time and at their own pace. There is always more to do than there are hands to do it, so if any of the following sounds like you, we would be glad to hear from you.

On site

Work days at the graveyard

Clearing brambles and ivy, cutting back, tidying paths, making leaning stones safe. Hand tools only and always carefully, because the stones are fragile. No experience needed — come for an hour or come for the morning. Tools, gloves and tea are provided.

From home

Records and research

Checking names against the census, birth and death indexes and the Welsh newspaper archives. Chasing down a date that does not quite add up. This can be done from a kitchen table anywhere in the world, and it is how most of our register was built.

Photography

Photographing headstones

Hundreds of the people in our register have no photograph of their memorial yet. If you are handy with a camera or even just a phone, a morning of careful photography adds something permanent that anyone searching for their family can see.

Welsh language

Reading and translating

Many of the older inscriptions are in Welsh, some of it worn and some of it verse. If you read Welsh, you can help us get the wording and the translations right — and tell us when we have got them wrong.

Groups

Scouts, schools and clubs

A group can take on a whole row or a corner of the ground and see a visible difference in a single session. We will show you what needs doing, work alongside you, and record what your group achieved so it is on the public record.

Skills

Something else you can do

Stonework, dry stone walling, joinery, gardening, fundraising, minute-taking, publicity, teaching, or simply knowing who to ask in Troedyrhiw. Small groups run on whatever skills turn up. Tell us what yours are.

What you get back

You can see what you did

A row that was under brambles in the morning is readable by the afternoon. Very little else in life gives you that.

Company, and a reason to be out of the house

A few hours of steady outdoor work with other people, at whatever pace suits you. Several of our volunteers came for the history and stayed for the company.

You learn how this work is really done

Reading a worn inscription, tracing a family through the records, handling old stone without damaging it. These are proper skills, and we will teach you them.

Names come back

Every inscription recorded is somebody's ancestor found by somebody else, sometimes from the other side of the world. We have had visitors from Patagonia.

Before you ask, the practical bits

  • There is no minimum commitment. Turn up once or turn up for years; both are useful.
  • You do not need to be from Troedyrhiw, and you do not need a family connection to Saron.
  • Nobody has to climb, lift or kneel. There is plenty of work that involves none of those.
  • The group carries insurance covering volunteers working on site.
  • Under-18s are welcome with a parent, carer or group leader.
  • We are not a registered charity — we are a community group with a constitution adopted at a public meeting in 2009, minuted meetings and accounts presented at each AGM.

A guide to the register

Meet Owain.

Owain knows this graveyard. Ask him about a name on a stone, a family in the register, or the story of the chapel itself — he'll answer from the project's own records, and tell you plainly when a stone is too worn to say.

Owain is imagined. The records are not. He is a character we created to give the archive a voice — there was never an Owain at Saron. What he knows comes from 867 transcribed burial records and the history published on these pages. He works only from those, and where the register is silent he says so rather than guess.

Stories behind the stones

Some of the people buried at Saron left more than a name and a date. A newspaper report, a chapel minute book, a photograph in a family album — enough, sometimes, to see a whole life. These are those people.

More stories are being prepared from the project's research files. If you know something about a name in the register, Owain can pass it on to the volunteers who look after the records.

Adopt a Grave

Take an interest in one grave

Saron is looked after by a handful of people, and there are more graves than they can reach. Adopting one means keeping an eye on it — the ground around the stone, the inscription staying readable — or finding out who the people named on it actually were. Either is worth doing. Both is better.

Two ways to help, and you can pick either

Nobody is asked to do both, and nobody is asked to commit to anything for longer than they want to. If you take a grave on and find you cannot keep it up, tell us and we will pass it to somebody else. No awkwardness.

On the ground

Maintenance

Keeping the grass and growth around the stone in check so the inscription stays visible and the plot does not disappear again. A few visits a year is enough for most graves. Nothing needs specialist tools, and nothing involving the stone itself should be attempted without speaking to us first — old stone is easier to damage than it looks.

From anywhere

Research

Following a name through newspapers, censuses, chapel records and family trees until a person comes into focus. This can be done entirely from home, from anywhere in the world, and it is how nearly every story on this site began. Several of our graves are still just a name and a date.

Why it matters

What it adds up to

Every grave someone looks after is one that a descendant can still find, read and photograph. People write to us from Canada, Australia and the United States looking for a Troedyrhiw ancestor. Whether they find anything depends entirely on whether somebody here kept the record and the stone in reach.

Where the graves are

Three areas have been surveyed closely enough for us to say with confidence which grave is which. You are welcome to adopt a single grave anywhere in them. But if no particular name is drawing you, we would gently suggest taking two or three that sit next to each other. A run of neighbouring graves is far less work than the same number scattered across the ground, and it leaves a visible stretch in good order rather than a patchwork.

Every grave we can identify

All of them are listed. There is no set order to work through and no route anybody has to follow — take the one that means something to you, or the one nearest where you park. Where a grave has already been taken you will see who looks after it.

How it works

  1. Tell us which grave Use the button on any grave above. It reaches the volunteers directly — nothing is published on the site and your details are not shown to anyone else.
  2. We check nobody else has it Then we send you everything we hold on that plot: the inscription as it was transcribed, the photograph if there is one, and anything already known about the people named.
  3. You go at your own pace There is no schedule and no reporting. Visit when you can, or research when you have an evening free.
  4. Anything you find comes back to the record Send us a photograph, a corrected date, a newspaper cutting, a family connection. It goes into the register under the grave, and your name goes beside it as the person who found it — unless you would rather it did not.

What about the rest of the graveyard?

There are several hundred more people we know were buried at Saron whose exact plot we have not yet fixed. Those graves cannot be adopted for maintenance, because we cannot yet point to which stone is which. But their research is wide open, and it is some of the most useful work anybody could do for this project. If a surname in the register means something to you, say so — you do not need a plot number to start.
